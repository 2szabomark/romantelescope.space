How fast is it going?

modeled

From a standstill on the pad to near escape speed in under an hour — then Earth's gravity claws almost all of it back. Roman arrives at L2 at roughly walking pace.

How to read it: the curve starts at zero on the pad; the launch spike shoots past the top of this scale and is clipped so the 90-day story stays readable. Gridlines every 2,000 km/h, the dashed vertical line marks today, amber is already flown. Modeled profile, not telemetry.

What will the camera see?

wakes in a few weeks

Roman's main camera is a wall of 18 infrared detectors — 300 megapixels per frame. The small square shows Hubble's entire field of view at the same sky scale.

Activation is planned a few weeks into the cruise; first public images by early 2027.

What happens next?

upcoming

Not just two burns and a parking spot. Below is the rest of the journey in five phases with their sub-steps — tap a phase to open it. The picture shows the part people rarely explain: why a halo orbit at all.

The loop redraws and Roman settles onto it, missing Earth's shadow the whole way round.
1. Finish unfoldingfirst days, early September
  • High-gain antenna deploysThe main dish unfolds at the rear. Until then Roman talks through smaller antennas; once it's open, the 1.4 TB-per-day science downlink is available.
  • Aperture cover opensThe visor lifts off the telescope's mouth — from here the optics see sky, and the barrel starts settling toward its cold operating state.
  • Coronagraph powers onThe planet-imaging technology demo boots up and begins health checks, years before its own observing campaign.
  • Trim burn 1A short thruster firing corrects the tiny aim error left over from the rocket. A small nudge now is far cheaper than a big burn later.
2. Cool down, wake the cameraweeks 2–5, September
  • Cooling and outgassing standard practiceIn vacuum, moisture trapped in materials slowly escapes and temperatures settle. Infrared detectors want deep cold and clean optics before going fully live.
  • Wide Field Instrument activatesA few weeks in, per NASA — the 300-megapixel camera and its 18 detectors switch on for the first time in space.
  • First detector readouts standard practiceDark frames, test patterns, first starlight on the chips. Engineering data — not public images yet.
3. The long middle of the cruiseweeks 5–12, October–November
  • Calibrations and testsNASA runs the instruments through a series of calibrations throughout the three-month commissioning — in practice that means focus sweeps, pointing-stability checks, filter wheels, detector behavior.
  • Trim burn 2The second and final course correction lines up the arrival geometry. NASA hasn't announced its date.
  • Slowing to a crawlBy November the model has Roman at bicycle pace, then walking pace relative to Earth — the last stretch is a drift, not a flight.
4. Arrival: onto the halo~day 90, late November
  • Braking burnWithout it, Roman would sail straight past. The burn bends the path onto the loop around L2.
  • Why a halo at allL2 itself is an unstable balance point sitting near the tip of Earth's shadow. Orbiting around the empty point keeps the solar panels in constant sunlight and the temperatures rock-steady.
  • Station-keeping begins standard practiceTiny periodic burns hold the loop — one reason nearly a ton of propellant is aboard. A single lap takes about half a year.
5. From first images to the surveysDecember, then 2027
  • End-to-end rehearsals standard practiceThe whole chain — point, observe, downlink 1.4 TB a day, process on the ground — gets exercised the way the surveys will run it.
  • First images releasedNASA anticipates early 2027. Expect a showpiece field that shows off the 100-times-Hubble view.
  • The science program startsFive years of wide surveys for dark energy and dark matter, a microlensing census of planets toward the galactic center, a supernova time-domain survey, and the coronagraph's planet-imaging demo — with machine learning and citizen scientists helping sift the data flood.

How to read it: bold sub-steps without a tag are NASA-stated; items marked "standard practice" describe how observatory commissioning usually runs — order and timing can differ. Week numbers and the arrival window come from this page's model.
